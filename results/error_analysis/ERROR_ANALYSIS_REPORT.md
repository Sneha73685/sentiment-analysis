# Depression v2 Models — Read-Only Error Analysis

Generated by `experiments/error_analysis_depression_v2.py`. Read-only: no
model was retrained or modified, no hyperparameters were tuned, and
`results/splits/depression_test.csv` was not modified or used to optimize
anything. Full numeric output lives in the accompanying CSVs and
`error_analysis_report.json`; this file is the narrative summary.

Test set: 1148 held-out rows (`results/splits/depression_test.csv`), never
seen during training or validation of either model. Confidence = the
model's own probability assigned to its predicted class (range 0.5–1.0).

correct.

## The single "strong disagreement" case

Only one row met the strict bar (both models >0.85 confidence, opposing
predictions):

> *"these day i don t feel like twittering don t know why"* — true label:
> **not_depressed**. Classical: depressed (conf 0.90, wrong). BERT:
> not_depressed (conf 0.999, correct).

Everywhere else, when the classical model disagrees with BERT, the
classical model's confidence in its own prediction is low (means around
0.62–0.65, barely above the 0.5 floor) — it is essentially never confident
*and* wrong at the same time as BERT is confident and right. That pattern
(35 of the 36 "classical wrong / BERT correct" cases) is directly supported
by the confidence-distribution numbers above.

## Confidence calibration observation (directly supported)

`error_confidence_distribution.png` shows BERT's confidence is extremely
concentrated near 1.0 **regardless of correctness** — its mean confidence
on its 18 wrong predictions (FP 0.991, FN 0.928) is nearly as high as on
correct ones. The classical model's errors, by contrast, cluster much
lower (0.52–0.73), closer to the decision boundary. This means: **BERT's
confidence score is a poor signal for catching its own errors**, while the
classical model's confidence score is comparatively more informative about
when it might be wrong. This is a direct, quantitative observation from
the data, not a proxy-based inference.

## Qualitative failure patterns (representative examples, truncated)

**Classical false positive, BERT correct** — context/idiom insensitivity:
> *"...if it had happened to anyone else i would have peed myself laughing
> i scraped my leg"* (true: not_depressed). Classical: depressed (0.52,
> barely over threshold). BERT: not_depressed (0.999). Bag-of-words TF-IDF
> has no way to distinguish this self-deprecating joke from genuine
> distress; BERT's contextual representation gets it right.

**BERT false negative, classical correct/wrong — apparent label-noise
cases:** Several of BERT's 16 false negatives are short posts whose text
does not obviously express depression, despite a "depressed" gold label
(the dataset labels by subreddit of origin, not per-utterance
annotation):
> *"just a motivational quote"* (true: depressed; both models predict
> not_depressed).
> *"anybody just let me know i m here to listen and meet new people"*
> (true: depressed; both models predict not_depressed, both with modest
> confidence 0.58–0.62).
> *"fuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuuck"* (true: depressed;
> both models predict not_depressed with high confidence).

These read like **source-level label noise** (r/depression-subreddit
membership used as ground truth rather than content-level annotation)
rather than model failure — see Limitations. This is an *observation*
(the examples exist and are representative of several similar cases in
`false_negatives_bert.csv`); the *label-noise explanation* is a hypothesis
that would require manual annotation to confirm.

## Linguistic pattern analysis (actually tested, not assumed)

11 heuristic patterns × 2 models = 22 Fisher's-exact tests were run (2×2:
pattern present/absent × correct/error). Full results with odds ratios and
p-values are in `linguistic_pattern_analysis.csv`. Bonferroni-corrected
significance threshold for 22 tests: **p < 0.00227**.

**No pattern survives Bonferroni correction.** Three patterns are nominally
significant at the uncorrected p < 0.05 level for the classical model:

| Pattern | Model | p-value | Error rate when flagged vs. unflagged |
|---|---|---|---|
| `long_context_heavy_input` (word count ≥ 90th pctile) | classical | 0.0071 | 0.0% vs 4.9% |
| `contextual_depression_mention_without_self_report` | classical | 0.0029 | 0.9% vs 5.3% |
| `contextual_depression_mention_without_self_report` | bert | 0.0332 | 0.0% vs 1.9% |
| `mixed_sentiment_language` (pos+neg words both present) | classical | 0.0294 | 0.0% vs 4.8% |

**Important direction note:** in every nominally-significant case, the
flagged texts have a *lower* error rate, not higher — i.e. long,
context-rich posts and posts that mention "depression" in a third-person/
topical way (e.g. discussing the subject, quoting others) are *easier* for
these models, not harder. This is the opposite of the naive expectation
that longer or more ambiguous text would be harder. Given none survive
multiple-comparison correction and BERT's error count is tiny (n=18), this
should be treated as a **weakly suggestive observation worth a dedicated
follow-up test**, not a established finding.

Patterns with **no evidence of association** (p > 0.05 for both models):
negation, uncertainty/hedging, third-person dominance, very-short-input,
temporary-affect language, sarcasm markers, metaphorical language.

**Construct-validity caveat (hypotheses, not findings):** `sarcasm_markers`,
`metaphorical_language`, `third_person_dominant`, and
`temporary_affect_language` are weak regex proxies with low expected
recall (documented per-pattern in `linguistic_pattern_analysis.csv`'s
`validity_note` column). Their null results are **not evidence that these
phenomena don't matter** — only evidence that these specific surface-form
proxies don't detect an effect. Testing this properly would require manual
annotation of a sarcasm/metaphor label, which is out of scope here.

## Limitations of this analysis

1. **BERT's error count is small (n=18)** — statistical power for any
   BERT-specific pattern association is low; a real effect could easily be
   missed (Type II error).
2. **Linguistic pattern flags are regexes, not validated classifiers** —
   several have low expected recall and were explicitly labeled as such;
   absence of a detected association is not proof of absence.
3. **22 simultaneous tests** inflate the chance of a spurious "significant"
   result at the uncorrected 0.05 level; the Bonferroni-corrected view (no
   survivors) is the more defensible read.
4. **Apparent label noise was not independently verified** — the
   label-noise hypothesis above rests on a handful of illustrative examples
   read by eye, not a systematic audit of the source dataset's labeling
   methodology.
5. **This is a single train/eval run** (one seed) — confidence-calibration
   and error-pattern findings have not been checked for stability across
   seeds.
6. **Confidence ≠ calibration** — "confidence" here is the raw softmax/
   logistic probability, not a calibrated estimate (no temperature scaling,
   Platt scaling, or reliability-diagram check was performed).
