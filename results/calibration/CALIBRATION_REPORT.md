# Depression v2 Models — Calibration & Uncertainty Analysis

Generated by `experiments/calibration_analysis_depression_v2.py`. Read-only
with respect to model weights: neither model was retrained or modified.
The only parameter fit anywhere in this analysis is BERT's scalar
temperature, learned exclusively on `results/splits/depression_val.csv`
and then frozen before ever touching the test set.

**Terminology, stated once and binding throughout:** "confidence" below
always means *the model's own predicted probability of its predicted
class* (a number between 0.5 and 1.0 for binary classification). It is
**not** a claim about clinical risk, true probability of depression, or
any ground truth about the person who wrote the text — it is a statement
about the model's internal certainty, nothing more.

## 1. Raw model confidence — test set, before any calibration

| | Classical (TF-IDF+LR) | BERT (raw, T=1.0) |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | 0.9556 | 0.9843 |
| Log-loss (NLL) | 0.2001 | 0.0796 |
| Brier score | 0.0487 [95% CI 0.0431–0.0546] | 0.0143 [95% CI 0.0081–0.0212] |
| ECE (10 bins, [0.5,1.0]) | **0.1033** [95% CI 0.0943–0.1156] | **0.0149** [95% CI 0.0083–0.0220] |
| Mean confidence, correct predictions | 0.862 | 0.998 |
| Mean confidence, incorrect predictions | 0.650 | **0.935** |

CIs are 95% bootstrap intervals (999 resamples, seed 42) over the fixed
1148-row test set.

**On its face, BERT's aggregate ECE (0.0149) is much lower than the
classical model's (0.1033) — BERT looks "better calibrated" overall.**
But this headline number is misleading on its own, for a specific,
data-supported reason — see §4.

## 2. BERT calibration before and after temperature scaling

Temperature was fit by minimizing log-loss on `depression_val.csv` logits
only (never on test): **T = 1.8148**. T > 1 softens BERT's probability
distribution, which is the expected direction for an overconfident
network per the temperature-scaling literature (Guo et al., 2017).

| | BERT raw (T=1.0) | BERT calibrated (T=1.815) |
|---|---|---|
| Accuracy | 0.9843 | 0.9843 (**identical**) |
| Log-loss | 0.0796 | **0.0605** |
| Brier score | 0.0143 [0.0081–0.0212] | **0.0136** [0.0079–0.0200] |
| ECE | 0.0149 [0.0083–0.0220] | **0.0108** [0.0056–0.0174] |
| Mean confidence, correct | 0.998 | 0.984 |
| Mean confidence, incorrect | 0.935 | 0.886 |

**Accuracy verified unchanged**: predicted classes for all 1148 test rows
are identical before and after scaling (checked directly, not just
inferred — temperature scaling with T>0 is a monotonic rescaling of
logits and cannot change an argmax, and this was confirmed empirically
rather than assumed).

Temperature scaling **improves all three calibration metrics** (log-loss,
Brier, ECE) — modestly, not dramatically, because the raw BERT model was
already fairly well calibrated in the aggregate sense. It also reduces
mean confidence on errors from 0.935 to 0.886 — a real but partial
correction; BERT's errors remain highly confident even after calibration.

## 3. Classification performance (unaffected by calibration)

Calibration changes *how much the model trusts its own predictions*, not
*what it predicts*. Accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and the confusion
matrix are identical to the Phase-3 official test results and are
reproduced here unchanged: Classical 0.9556 acc / 0.9537 F1; BERT 0.9843
acc / 0.9838 F1 (see `results/depression_v2_model_comparison.csv`). This
analysis adds calibration/uncertainty metrics on top of, not instead of,
those numbers.

## 4. Uncertainty / calibration performance — the key nuance

**BERT is simultaneously "well calibrated in aggregate" AND "overconfident
specifically when wrong." Both are true, and they are not contradictory —
they answer different questions.**

Evidence, directly from the reliability bins (`calibration_report.json`):

- 1138 of 1148 test predictions (99.1%) fall in BERT's top confidence bin
  [0.95, 1.0], where avg confidence is 0.9992 and observed accuracy is
  0.9877. The gap is small (~0.011) but since this single bin holds
  essentially the entire test set, it dominates the ECE computation and
  keeps the aggregate ECE low.
- Within that same bin sit **all but a handful of BERT's 18 errors** —
  each one individually near-certain (mean confidence on BERT's errors:
  0.935 raw, 0.886 calibrated). A metric like ECE, which averages
  calibration gaps *weighted by bin population*, cannot surface this: 18
  confidently-wrong predictions barely move a population-weighted average
  when 1130 confidently-right predictions sit in the same bin.
- The classical model shows the **opposite** pattern: it is mostly
  *underconfident*, not overconfident. Its reliability diagram
  (`reliability_diagram_classical.png`) shows observed accuracy exceeding
  average confidence in nearly every bin above 0.55 (e.g. bin
  [0.55,0.60): avg confidence 0.577, observed accuracy 0.758; bin
  [0.90,0.95): avg confidence 0.924, observed accuracy 0.992). This
  systematic underconfidence is what drives its higher ECE (0.1033) —
  not overconfidence on errors, but a general habit of hedging even when
  it turns out to be right.

**Conclusion supported directly by the data**: yes, BERT is confident on
its incorrect predictions (mean confidence 0.935 raw / 0.886 calibrated on
its 18 errors) — this confirms the concern raised in the prior error
analysis. This is compatible with BERT also having good *aggregate* ECE,
because aggregate ECE is dominated by the enormous majority-correct top
bin and is not designed to detect this specific failure mode (a small
number of high-confidence errors embedded in an otherwise accurate,
high-confidence bin). **If the downstream use case cares about catching
individual confident-but-wrong predictions (e.g. flagging low-trust
outputs for review), aggregate ECE is the wrong metric to optimize for —
mean confidence on likely-error subsets is more informative.** This is an
observation from the data, not a statistical test — no formal test was
run for "is confidence-on-errors different between models" beyond the
descriptive means and their CIs' non-overlap-based intuition below.

## Classical model: calibration recommendation (evidence only, not applied)

Per instructions, the classical model was **not** calibrated — only
diagnosed. Val-set ECE (0.0982) and test-set ECE (0.1033) are consistent
with each other (similar magnitude, both well above the near-zero ECE a
well-calibrated model would show), and the miscalibration direction
(underconfidence in the 0.55–0.95 confidence range) is consistent across
both splits. **Recommendation: this model would likely benefit from
calibration (e.g. Platt scaling or isotonic regression fit on the
validation set) in a future phase.** This is a recommendation backed by
the val/test evidence above, not an automatic action — no calibration was
fit or applied to the classical model anywhere in this analysis.

## Limitations

1. **BERT's error count is small (n=18)** — the mean-confidence-on-errors
   statistic (0.935 raw) rests on only 18 data points; no formal
   hypothesis test was run to compare it against classical's
   mean-confidence-on-errors (0.650), only descriptive comparison. Treat
   the magnitude as indicative, not statistically certified.
2. **BERT's reliability diagram bins below 0.9 confidence contain very
   few samples** (often 1–3 per bin) — their plotted "accuracy" bars can
   show 0% or 100% from a single data point and should not be
   over-interpreted visually; the bin-count subplot under each reliability
   diagram exists specifically so this sparsity is visible rather than
   hidden.
3. **ECE with 10 fixed-width bins is one specific calibration metric**
   among several (adaptive-width binning, MCE, class-wise ECE would give
   different pictures, particularly given how concentrated BERT's
   confidence distribution is). The bin edges and count are a documented
   design choice, not a neutral default.
4. **Temperature scaling is a single global parameter** — it can't correct
   miscalibration that varies by input type or confidence region (e.g. the
   residual gap visible in the calibrated BERT reliability diagram's
   [0.90, 0.95) bin, where accuracy is still notably below confidence even
   after scaling).
5. **Single train/eval run, one seed** — calibration metrics were not
   checked for stability across retraining seeds.
6. **No clinical validity claim of any kind** — nothing in this analysis
   should be read as evidence about depression risk, diagnostic accuracy,
   or real-world reliability of these models for any clinical or
   quasi-clinical purpose. It measures model self-reported confidence
   against model correctness on a fixed benchmark dataset, nothing more.
